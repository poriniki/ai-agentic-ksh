**On slide:**

# Module 1 — How AI really works (0:15–0:45)

## Slide 8 — What a language model actually does

**Time:** 0:15–0:19 · **Type:** Concept

**On slide:**
- It **predicts** the most likely next words, based on patterns from huge amounts of text
- It is **not** a database that looks things up
- It is **not** a calculator (unless it uses a tool)
- It does **not** know your company, unless you tell it

**Visual:** "The quarterly results show a ___" with three candidate words and probability bars.

**Speaker notes:**
Keep it non-technical. The model was trained on a huge library of text and learned which words tend to follow which. When you ask a question, it writes the answer that *sounds most like* a good answer. Most of the time, what sounds right *is* right, because the patterns are rich. But "sounds right" and "is right" are different things, and everything else today comes from that difference. Reference: Andrew Ng's *Generative AI for Everyone* (in the resources) explains this well.

---

## Slide 9 — Three consequences you must remember

**Time:** 0:19–0:23 · **Type:** Concept

**On slide:**
1. **Fluent ≠ correct.** Confidence in the tone tells you nothing about accuracy.
2. **It only knows what is in front of it.** Missing context gets filled with plausible guesses.
3. **It wants to be helpful.** If you ask for 10 sources, it may produce 10, real or not.

**Visual:** Three cards, each with a short example in small text.

**Speaker notes:**
Example for #1: it will state a market size with the same calm tone whether it is right or invented. Example for #2: ask for a "campaign plan" with no budget, audience or market and you get a generic plan. The AI did not fail; the brief did. Example for #3: asking for a fixed number of things pressures it to fill the list. The fix is to give it permission: "If you are not sure, say so." These three ideas are the basis for *Discernment* in the next module.

## Slide 10 — The context window: the AI's desk

**Time:** 0:23–0:26 · **Type:** Concept

**On slide:**
- Everything the AI "knows" about your task is what is on its **desk**: your messages, files, instructions
- **New chat = empty desk**
- Very long chats get messy: early details can be missed
- Projects and memory = a desk that is **already set up** when you sit down

**Visual:** A desk illustration with papers (prompt, file, instructions); a second, empty desk labelled "new chat".

**Speaker notes:**
This is the most useful metaphor of the day. It explains why re-typing context is necessary (and why Projects fix it), why a 200-message chat starts to drift, and why "it forgot what I said" happens. Practical rule: one chat per task; when a chat gets long or confused, ask for a summary, then start a new chat and paste the summary.

## Slide 11 — Knowledge cutoff and tools

**Time:** 0:26–0:29 · **Type:** Concept

**On slide:**
- The model's built-in knowledge stops at a **training cutoff date**
- **Tools** extend it:
  - Web search / Research → current information *with sources*
  - File upload → *your* documents
  - Code execution / analysis → real calculations on data
  - Connectors → company tools (drive, email, calendar…)
- Always ask: *is this answer coming from memory or from a tool?*

**Visual:** A central "model" circle with four tool icons around it.

**Speaker notes:**
Without tools, the model answers from memory, which may be out of date or wrong. With web search it can quote current sources, which you can then check. With data analysis it actually computes rather than "guessing" numbers. Pro habit: for anything factual or numerical, make it use a tool and show where the answer came from.

## Slide 12 — Live demo: confident and wrong

**Time:** 0:29–0:35 · **Type:** Live demo

**On slide:**
- Same question, two ways:
  1. No tools: "What is the average email open rate in [our industry] in [country], with 3 sources?"
  2. With web search: same question, plus "only use sources you can link to; say if you can't find data"
- Let's check the sources together

**Visual:** Screen share.

**Speaker notes:**
Prepare this in advance and test it (see facilitator guide, Demo 1). Pick a niche, specific question from your industry or market; niche questions bring out errors more reliably. In run 1, look for specific numbers and sources that sound real. Click or search for them live. In run 2, show the difference: links, hedging, maybe "limited data". If run 1 happens to be correct, that is fine too: the lesson is that *you couldn't tell without checking*. Ask the room: "Would you have put run 1 in a board deck?"

## Slide 13 — The jagged frontier

**Time:** 0:35–0:39 · **Type:** Concept

**On slide:**

| Usually strong | Often weak |
| --- | --- |
| Drafting, rewriting, changing tone | Precise facts, recent events (without search) |
| Summarising and structuring messy text | Exact numbers (without a calculation tool) |
| Brainstorming options and angles | Your company's context and politics |
| Explaining concepts at any level | Knowing what it doesn't know |
| Critiquing a draft against criteria | Long chains of reasoning with no checks |
| Turning notes into structure | Legal, tax and regulatory specifics |

**Visual:** A jagged line separating "inside" and "outside" the frontier.

**Speaker notes:**
"Jagged frontier" is from Ethan Mollick and research with BCG consultants: AI is excellent at some tasks and surprisingly bad at others that look similar in difficulty. In that research, consultants using AI did better on tasks inside the frontier and *worse* on a task outside it, because they trusted it. The skill is knowing which side of the line your task is on. Ask each team to name one task they think is inside the frontier and one outside. Collect 2–3 answers.

## Slide 14 — The right mental model: a brilliant intern with amnesia

**Time:** 0:39–0:43 · **Type:** Core metaphor

**On slide:**
Think of AI as a new intern who is…
- extremely fast and very well-read
- eager to please (and sometimes bluffs)
- **knows nothing about your company**
- **forgets everything** when the conversation ends

**Visual:** A friendly intern illustration with a "Day 1" badge.

This metaphor tells you what to do. You wouldn't send an intern's first draft straight to a client, so you review. You wouldn't say "do the marketing thing", so you brief. You'd give them the brand guide, so you set up a Project. You'd check their numbers, so you verify. Everything in Modules 2–4 is how you'd manage a brilliant intern. # Module 2 — The 4Ds and the pro brief (0:45–1:35)

## Slide 16 — The 4Ds of AI fluency

**Time:** 0:45–0:49 · **Type:** Core framework

**On slide:**

| D | Question | In practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Delegation** | *Should* AI do this, and which part? | Choose the task and the split between you and AI |
| **Description** | Have I explained it clearly? | Goal, context, format, examples |
| **Discernment** | Is this output actually good? | Check facts, logic, gaps, tone |
| **Diligence** | Am I using it responsibly? | Data, transparency, ownership |

Source: Anthropic Academy, *AI Fluency: Framework & Foundations*

**Visual:** Four quadrants, one per D.

**Speaker notes:**
This framework comes from Anthropic's free AI Fluency course (link in resources) and works with any AI tool. It's a cycle: decide what to delegate, describe it well, judge the output, and act responsibly. Each of the next slides takes one D. Tell them the homework and project rubrics are built on these four.

## Slide 17 — Delegation: the Delegation Matrix

**Time:** 0:49–0:53 · **Type:** Core framework

**On slide:**

| AI does it | AI drafts, you review | You do it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low stakes, easy to check | Medium/high stakes, AI speeds you up | Needs judgment, relationships or accountability |
| Reformat, summarise, first ideas | Reports, proposals, analysis, copy | Final decisions, negotiations, sensitive messages |

Ask three questions:
1. What happens if it's wrong? (**stakes**)
2. Can I check it quickly? (**verifiability**)
3. Does it need knowledge only I have? (**tacit context**)

**Visual:** Three columns, green / amber / grey.

**Speaker notes:**
This is the second core tool of the day. Delegation is not "all or nothing": you split a task into parts and delegate each part at a different level. Most professional work sits in the middle column. "You do it" doesn't mean AI is useless there: it can still help you *prepare* (practise a negotiation, list objections), but the act and the accountability are yours.

## Slide 19 — Description: the 6-part brief

**Time:** 0:56–1:00 · **Type:** Core framework

**On slide:**

| Part | Answers | Example phrase |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Who** | Role for the AI + audience | "Act as a senior B2B copywriter. Readers are CFOs of mid-size firms." |
| **Why** | Goal and what success looks like | "Goal: get them to book a demo." |
| **What** | The task and the input | "Write 3 email subject lines + body for the product notes below." |
| **How** | Format, length, tone, structure | "Under 120 words, plain language, one CTA, a table at the end." |
| **Like this** | Examples of good output | "Here are 2 emails that performed well: …" |
| **Not this** | Constraints and things to avoid | "No hype words, no discounts, don't invent features." |

**Visual:** Six blocks forming a brief "form".

**Speaker notes:**
This is the core prompt framework for the day; it's on the prompt library handout. You don't need all six every time; for important or recurring tasks, use all six. "Like this" (examples) is the most underrated part: one good example often does more than a paragraph of instructions. "Not this" is where you prevent the things that annoy you about AI output.

## Slide 20 — Before / after: Marketing

**Time:** 1:00–1:02 · **Type:** Example

**On slide:**

**Before:**
> Write a LinkedIn post about our new product.

**After:**
> You are a B2B social media writer for [Company], a [industry] firm. Audience: operations managers at mid-size manufacturers in [country].
> Goal: drive sign-ups to our webinar on [date].
> Write 2 alternative LinkedIn posts about [product] using the product notes below.
> Each under 150 words, first line must work as a hook, one clear CTA, max 3 hashtags.
> Match the tone of this past post that performed well: """[example]"""
> Don't use "revolutionary", "game-changer" or emojis. Don't claim features not in the notes.
> Product notes: """[notes]"""

**Visual:** Side by side, the "after" with the six parts colour-coded.

**Speaker notes:**
Point to each colour and name the part. If time allows, show both outputs live (prepared beforehand). The "after" took 2 minutes longer to write and saves 15 minutes of editing. Mention the triple quotes: they separate *your instructions* from *pasted material*, so the AI doesn't confuse them. Claude also responds well to XML-style tags like `<notes>…</notes>`.

## Slide 21 — Before / after: Finance and Business

**Time:** 1:02–1:04 · **Type:** Example

**On slide:**

**Finance before:** "Explain the variance in this table."
**Finance after:** "You are an FP&A analyst writing for the executive team, who are not finance specialists. Using the attached budget-vs-actual table, identify the 3 largest variances by absolute value, explain the likely drivers *only using information in the table or my notes*, and flag anything you cannot explain as 'needs input'. Output: a 5-bullet summary, then a table (line item, variance, % variance, driver, confidence). Show the calculation for each variance."

**Business before:** "Summarise this meeting."
**Business after:** "Turn these meeting notes into: (1) decisions made, (2) action items with owner and date, (3) open questions, (4) risks. Use only what is in the notes; mark anything ambiguous with [?]. Format as a table I can paste into an email."

**Visual:** Two before/after pairs.

**Speaker notes:**
Highlight two pro moves in the finance example: "only using information in the table" (limits invention) and "flag anything you cannot explain" (gives it permission to say it doesn't know). "Show the calculation" makes it checkable. The business example shows that *format* is half the value: an output structured for its next use.

## Slide 22 — Six techniques that raise quality

**Time:** 1:04–1:08 · **Type:** Techniques

**On slide:**
1. **Give examples** of what good looks like
2. **Separate** instructions from pasted material (`"""` or `<tags>`)
3. **Ask it to plan first:** "Before writing, outline your approach"
4. **Allow "I don't know":** "If information is missing, ask or say so"
5. **Ask for options:** "Give 3 different approaches with pros and cons"
6. **Ask it to check itself:** "Review your answer against my requirements and list any gaps"

**Visual:** Six icons in a grid.

**Speaker notes:**
These come from Anthropic's prompt engineering guide and the Vanderbilt prompt patterns course (both in resources). #3 improves complex tasks because the model "thinks on paper" before it answers. #5 is great for strategy work: you choose instead of accepting the first idea. #6 helps, but it is **not** a replacement for your own check; the model can miss its own mistakes.

## Slide 23 — It's a conversation, not a vending machine

**Time:** 1:08–1:11 · **Type:** Concept

**On slide:**
- The first answer is a **first draft**
- Give **specific** feedback: "Paragraph 2 is too formal; cut the second option; add a number for the cost saving"
- Avoid vague feedback: "make it better"
- Start a **new chat** when:
  - the chat is long or drifting
  - you're switching to a different task
  - it keeps repeating a mistake
- Before starting fresh: "Summarise what we agreed so far" → paste into the new chat

**Visual:** A loop: brief → draft → specific feedback → better draft.

**Speaker notes:**
Most people give up after one or two tries or keep saying "again". Specific feedback is the same skill as managing people. Also share the edit-and-resend trick: editing your earlier message often works better than adding a correction on top, because it removes the bad turn from the context.

## Slide 24 — Discernment: checking the output

**Time:** 1:11–1:15 · **Type:** Checklist

**On slide:**
Before you use any AI output, check:
- [ ] **Facts:** are names, dates, numbers and sources real?
- [ ] **Numbers:** recalculate at least the key ones
- [ ] **Logic:** do the conclusions follow from the evidence?
- [ ] **Gaps:** what is missing that an expert would expect?
- [ ] **Fit:** right tone, audience, format and length?
- [ ] **Bias:** is it one-sided or stereotyped?

The more it matters, the deeper the check.

**Visual:** Checklist with a magnifying glass.

**Speaker notes:**
Discernment means judging the output, the process (how it reached the answer) and its behaviour (did it follow your instructions?). A useful trick: ask it to "list the claims in your answer that I should verify, ranked by risk". It's good at pointing you to the weak spots. But the check itself is yours. Finance people are usually good at this already; ask one of them how they check a junior's spreadsheet. It's the same skill.

- **Transparency:** be open about AI use where it matters (clients, reports, decisions)
- **Fairness:** watch for bias in anything about people

## Slide 26 — Exercise 1: From one-liner to pro brief

**Time:** 1:18–1:30 · **Type:** Exercise (12 min)

**On slide:**
1. Pick a task **you do every week** (2 min)
2. Write the one-line prompt you'd normally use → run it → save the output (3 min)
3. Rewrite it with the **6-part brief** in a **new chat** → run it (5 min)
4. Compare: what changed? How much editing would each need? (2 min)

Use sample or public data only.

## Slide 31 — Projects: the AI's desk, already set up

**Time:** 1:50–1:54 · **Type:** Feature

**On slide:**
A Project = **knowledge** (files) + **instructions** (how to behave) + **all related chats**
- Set up once per recurring area of work
- Every new chat in the Project starts with that context
- Shareable with the team (on team plans)

Good Project ideas:
- "Brand & Content" · "Monthly Management Report" · "Client X Proposals" · "Board Pack"

**Visual:** A Project "box" containing files, an instructions sheet and chat threads.

**Speaker notes:**
This is the single most valuable feature for this audience. Rule of thumb: if you've explained the same background to the AI three times, it should be a Project. Show (or pre-record) the Project screen and point to where files and instructions go.

## Slide 32 — What goes into a Project

**Time:** 1:54–1:57 · **Type:** Guidance

**On slide:**

| Put in | Keep out |
| --- | --- |
| Brand/style guide, tone of voice | Personal data, salaries, HR cases |
| Product or service descriptions | Unreleased financials (unless approved) |
| Glossary of internal terms | Passwords, contracts with confidential terms |
| Templates and formats you use | Anything the data rules mark red |
| 2–3 examples of excellent past work | Outdated documents (they confuse it) |
| Process descriptions, checklists | Huge dumps of everything "just in case" |

**Visual:** Two columns: green tick / red cross.

**Speaker notes:**
Quality of context beats quantity. Ten focused files work better than 200 random ones. Outdated files are dangerous: the AI will follow old pricing as happily as new pricing. Assign an owner to keep each team Project up to date (Module 6).

## Slide 33 — Writing Project instructions

## Slide 34 — Files and data analysis

**Time:** 2:01–2:05 · **Type:** Feature

**On slide:**
- Upload documents → ask questions, compare, extract, summarise
- Upload spreadsheets/CSV → the AI **writes and runs code** to calculate and chart
- Pro habits:
  - "Describe the data first: columns, rows, missing values, oddities"
  - "Show the method / formulas you used"
  - Recompute 2–3 key numbers yourself
  - Ask for the output **as a file** (Excel, CSV, chart)

**Visual:** Spreadsheet icon → chart icon, with "show your method" in the middle.

**Speaker notes:**
Explain the difference from Slide 8: when the AI runs code on a file, it actually calculates instead of predicting numbers, so arithmetic is far more reliable. But it can still pick the wrong column, misread a date format or exclude rows. That's why "describe the data first" is the first step. It catches misunderstandings before any analysis.

## Slide 36 — Research with sources

# Module 4 — Think in workflows, not prompts (2:25–3:05)

## Slide 39 — The pro shift: from tasks to processes

**Time:** 2:25–2:28 · **Type:** Transition

**On slide:**
- Amateur: "What prompt should I use for my monthly report?"
- Pro: "What are the **steps** of my monthly report, and which ones should AI do?"
- One giant prompt → mediocre result
- Several focused steps with checks → reliable result

**Visual:** One big messy box vs a row of small neat boxes with checkpoints.

**Speaker notes:**
This is the most important mindset shift of the day and the basis of the project. A complex task in a single prompt asks the AI to do everything at once with no chance to correct it. Breaking it into steps gives you control and much better quality. This is Level 4 on the ladder.

---

## Slide 40 — Process mapping: an example

**Time:** 2:28–2:33 · **Type:** Worked example

**On slide:**
**Monthly performance report**

| # | Step | Who | Check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Export data from systems | You | — |
| 2 | Clean and check data quality | AI does it | Spot-check 5 rows |
| 3 | Calculate KPIs and variances | AI does it (code) | Recompute 3 numbers |
| 4 | Identify top 3 insights | AI drafts → you review | Do they match what you know? |
| 5 | Write commentary | AI drafts → you review | Tone, accuracy |
| 6 | Critique as the CFO would | AI does it | — |
| 7 | Final edit and sign-off | You | ✓ |

**Visual:** A horizontal flow with colour-coded steps (green / amber / grey from the Delegation Matrix).

**Speaker notes:**
Walk through each step and name the column: AI does it, AI drafts and you review, or you do it. Notice: (1) the human is at the start and the end, (2) every AI step has a matching check, (3) step 6 uses AI to *critique*, a powerful use. Run the workflow twice, then improve the weakest step. That is exactly the individual project.

## Slide 41 — Five workflow patterns (in plain language)

**Time:** 2:33–2:39 · **Type:** Core framework

**On slide:**

| Pattern | Plain-language picture | Business example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Chaining** | Assembly line: each step feeds the next | Research → outline → draft → edit |
| **Routing** | Triage desk: sort first, then handle by type | Classify customer emails → different reply templates |
| **Parallelisation** | Several people work at once, or several reviewers vote | Analyse 5 competitors separately, then combine |
| **Orchestrator–workers** | A manager splits the job and assigns parts | "Plan this launch" → AI breaks it into workstreams |
| **Evaluator–optimiser** | Writer + editor loop | Draft → critique against rubric → revise |

Source: Anthropic, *Building effective agents*

**Visual:** Five small diagrams, one per pattern.

**Speaker notes:**
These come from Anthropic's "Building effective agents" article, written for engineers, but the ideas work for anyone. You can run all five by hand in a chat interface today. Chaining and evaluator–optimiser are the two they'll use most. Ask: "Which of these do you already do without naming it?" Many teams already do routing and parallel review by hand.

## Slide 42 — Evaluator–optimiser in practice

**Time:** 2:39–2:43 · **Type:** Technique

**On slide:**
1. Write your **quality criteria** first (a rubric)
2. AI **drafts**
3. In a **new chat** (fresh eyes), AI **critiques** the draft against the rubric, as a specific reader
   > "You are a sceptical CFO. Score this proposal 1–5 on each criterion and list the 3 biggest weaknesses."
4. AI (or you) **revises**
5. Stop after 2–3 rounds, then **you** do the final review

**Visual:** A loop between "Writer" and "Critic", with a rubric in the middle.

**Speaker notes:**
Why a new chat for the critique? A model reviewing its own text in the same chat tends to be too kind to it. Role-playing a critical reader ("sceptical CFO", "busy customer", "competitor") gives better feedback. This works very well for proposals, copy, reports and presentations, and is required in both projects.

## Slide 43 — Workflows vs agents

**Time:** 2:43–2:47 · **Type:** Concept

**On slide:**
- **Workflow:** *you* decide the steps; AI does them
- **Agent:** *AI* decides the steps, uses tools, and loops until done; you set the goal and the limits
- Agents are good when the path is unclear and the steps are valuable but hard to script
- Agents cost more time and need more supervision
- **Start with the simplest thing that works.** Add autonomy only where it pays off.

**Visual:** Left: a fixed flowchart (workflow). Right: a loop "think → act with tool → observe → repeat" (agent).

**Speaker notes:**
Agent features now exist in mainstream tools: Claude's Cowork can do multi-step tasks on files, ChatGPT has agent mode, and research modes are agents too. The advice from Anthropic's engineers (Barry Zhang's talk, in resources) is to *not* reach for an agent first. A clear workflow you understand beats an autonomous agent you can't check. For this audience: master Level 4 before going to Level 5.

---

## Slide 44 — Where to put the human

**Time:** 2:47–2:50 · **Type:** Principles

**On slide:**
Always put a human checkpoint:
- **Before anything leaves the company** (clients, public, regulators)
- **Before money moves** or numbers are committed
- **Before decisions about people**
- **Where errors compound** (a wrong number in step 2 ruins step 7)
- **At the end:** someone owns the result

**Visual:** A workflow line with "stop sign" icons at checkpoints.

**Speaker notes:**
Connect this to Diligence. The checkpoint after early data steps is the most often forgotten and the most important. An unchecked error early in a chain spreads into everything after it. When supervising an agent, the checkpoints are the moments you review its plan and its results.

# Module 5 — Let AI ask the questions (3:15–3:35)

## Slide 48 — Flip the interaction

**Time:** 3:15–3:18 · **Type:** Concept

**On slide:**
- Usually: **you** ask, AI answers, using guesses where you left gaps
- Flipped: **AI asks you** until it has what it needs, *then* answers
- Best for: vague ideas, new projects, briefs, requirements, decisions
- It makes your own thinking clearer, often the biggest benefit

**Visual:** Two arrows, user→AI and AI→user, with the second highlighted.

**Speaker notes:**
This is the "Flipped Interaction" pattern from the Vanderbilt prompt engineering course. Remember Slide 9: missing context gets filled with guesses. Instead of trying to remember everything to put in the brief, let the AI find the gaps. People are often surprised how much this clarifies their own thinking. It's like a good consultant asking questions before giving advice.

---

## Slide 49 — Four prompts for discovery

**Time:** 3:18–3:23 · **Type:** Techniques

**On slide:**
1. **Interview me first**
   > "Before answering, ask me one question at a time until you have what you need to [goal]. Stop after at most 8 questions, then summarise what you learned."
2. **Assumption check**
   > "List the assumptions you're making about my situation, and the question that would confirm or break each."
3. **Notes to requirements**
   > "From these meeting notes, list goals, requirements, constraints and open questions. Quote the line each item came from."
4. **Gap hunt**
   > "What is missing, unclear or contradictory in this plan? Rank by risk."

**Visual:** Four cards with the prompts.

**Speaker notes:**
These four are on the prompt library handout. "One question at a time" is important: a list of 15 questions at once is overwhelming and gets short answers. "Quote the line" in #3 is a Discernment technique: it lets you trace each requirement back to its source and catch invented ones. #4 is excellent before any big meeting or decision.

## Slide 50 — Exercise 3: Interview in pairs

**Time:** 3:23–3:31 · **Type:** Exercise (8 min)

**On slide:**
In pairs:
- **Person A:** has a vague idea (a campaign, a new report, a process change, a budget request)
- A uses the **"interview me first"** prompt and answers the AI's questions
- **Person B** watches: which questions were useful? Which would *you* have asked?
- At the end: ask the AI for a **one-page brief** (goal, audience, requirements, risks, open questions)

**Visual:** Two people icon + timer.

**Speaker notes:**
Eight minutes is enough for one run; if they're fast, they swap roles. Pairing people from different teams works well: a finance person watching a marketing interview spots different gaps. Remind them it's fine to answer "I don't know yet". That itself is a finding.
